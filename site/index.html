How much the arrival date moved

Each row is one bird. The hollow dot is where its arrival date sat in the early window; the filled dot is the late window. A line running leftwards means the bird now arrives earlier in the year.

    The two resident birds sit far to the left, and that is not a mistake. A bird that is here all year has already been seen thousands of times by mid-January, so this method says it “arrives” in January. For them, only the length of the line means anything, and it should be nothing.

    How this was measured

    Where the numbers come from

    Every sighting here is a birdwatcher's eBird checklist entry, made public through GBIF, recorded in California with map coordinates. One dataset only, for both time windows, so a change in who publishes data cannot masquerade as a change in when birds arrive.

    Why not the first sighting of spring?

    Because it mostly measures birdwatchers. Far more people use eBird now than in 2010. With more people out looking, somebody catches the front of the migration sooner, even if the birds are doing exactly what they always did. Compare a first-sighting date from 2010 with one from 2024 and you will find “earlier arrival” that is really “more binoculars”.

    What we use instead

    For each bird, in each spring, we take the day by which one tenth of that year's sightings had happened. Think of the spring's sightings as a crowd filing through a door: the first person through is a fluke, but the moment the first tenth are through is a property of the crowd. Doubling the number of people does not move it.

    Each five-year window gets the median of its five yearly dates, and the shift is the late window minus the early one. A species-year with fewer than 100 sightings is dropped, because a tenth of twelve sightings is just the second sighting wearing a disguise.

    What the control tells us

    A percentile is much steadier than a first sighting, but it is not immune. If the extra observers bird at different times or in different places, it moves too. That is what the two resident birds are for. They cannot arrive earlier, so their shift is a direct measurement of how much this method drifts on its own, and it is subtracted from the headline.

    What this cannot tell you

    • There are far more birdwatchers now. The percentile and the control birds are both attempts to handle this, and neither is a complete fix. Treat a shift smaller than the control's shift as noise.
    • Sightings are not a count of birds. They are a count of times somebody wrote a bird down. A species can become easier to find without becoming more numerous.
    • California is large. A statewide average hides the coast behaving differently from the Central Valley and the Sierra. A bird can arrive earlier in one and later in another and show nothing here.
    • Some of these birds spend the winter here. A January sighting of a bird that never left is not an arrival. Species where this swamps the signal are dropped and named; species where it is mild are marked. The decision was made from the monthly counts, not from memory.
    • Two windows is not a trend. This compares one five-year block against another. It cannot tell you whether the change was steady, sudden, or is still going on.
    • Nothing here explains why. Earlier arrival is consistent with warming springs, but this project measures timing and nothing else. It offers no evidence about the cause.
